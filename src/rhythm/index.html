How a session feels

The Rhythm of Play

A D&D session looks complicated from outside and turns out to be one small loop, repeated. Learn the loop and you can follow any table. After that, the skill is knowing when to speed up and when to slow down.

01 · The loop

Describe, answer, resolve

The Dungeon Master’s Guide breaks every encounter into three steps. Almost everything you do at the table fits one of them.

  1. The DM describes the situation

    The DM tells you what your characters can see and hear. Everything you know about the world comes from here. A few sentences is enough; the DM adds more as you look closer.

  2. The players say what they do

    Each player answers for their own character: open the chest, or go through the door. Outside a fight there are no turns. In a fight, everyone acts in Initiative order.

  3. The DM describes what happens

    Sometimes nothing stands in the way and the DM just says it happens. If something does, the DM asks for a D20 Test, or plays the creature you are talking to. In a fight, the combat rules take over.

What happens next gives you a new situation. Back to step 1.

02 · Scenes and cuts

A session is a run of scenes

The Guide compares a session to a film: a series of encounters with cuts between them. An encounter can be exploration (including puzzles), a conversation, or a fight. When one ends, the DM moves on to the next.

The cuts matter as much as the scenes. A long road or a night of rest that nobody interrupts can each be one sentence. The Guide’s advice is to skip the mundane parts and get back to the action. Quiet stretches are part of the design, because they make the loud parts land.

03 · How much fits

About three things an hour

The Guide’s rule of thumb is that most groups get through at least three things in an hour. A thing can be exploring a chamber, talking to someone, settling an argument, solving a riddle, surviving a trap or winning an easy fight. A harder fight counts as two or three. A tense negotiation can fill the whole hour. Fill the hour below.

04 · Keeping time

Habits that keep it moving

  • Start

    Open with a recap

    After the first session, begin by recalling what happened last time. The DM can do it quickly. Or the players can, which shows the DM what they remember and what they cared about.

  • Describe

    Short first, longer on request

    Players tend to drift after about three sentences of description. Give the first view briefly and save the rest for when someone opens the drawer or looks behind the curtain.

  • Ask

    Everyone gets a turn to answer

    Even outside combat, check in with each player so quiet ones are heard. A group answer is fine, and so is two players splitting up to do two things.

  • Cut

    Gloss over the dull parts

    If a rest passes without incident, say so and move on. Nobody needs a vote on who cooks dinner unless the table enjoys it.

  • Breathe

    Break after the heavy scene

    After a long fight or a tense conversation, take a short break. The DM can leave the players in suspense while they work out what comes next.

  • End

    End on a hook

    Try not to stop in the middle of a fight, since the Initiative order is hard to rebuild a week later. A deliberate cliffhanger works well. In the last few minutes, ask what people liked and what they want more of.

05 · At your seat

What a player can do for the rhythm

  • Say what you want to do

    One sentence is plenty. “I check the door for traps” gives the DM everything needed to run it.

  • Ask questions about the scene

    If you cannot picture the room, ask. The DM chose what to say first, and you can always get more.

  • Plan while others act

    Think about your next move while others act, so your turn takes seconds. Long planning talks among the characters are normal, and the DM will step back from them.
